PILLAR 1 Core Pathophysiology & Metabolic Adaptation
1.1

Starvation vs. Stress Metabolism Pathways

The body responds to nutritional deprivation differently depending on whether it is in a stressed or non-stressed state:

  • Simple Starvation (Non-stressed):
    • 0 – 24 Hours: Hepatic glycogenolysis maintains serum glucose. These reserves are depleted within 24 hours.
    • 24 Hours – 5 Days: Gluconeogenesis begins in the liver and kidneys, using amino acids (alanine from skeletal muscle proteolysis) and glycerol (from adipose tissue lipolysis) to maintain glucose delivery to the brain and red blood cells.
    • > 5 Days (Ketosis Adaptation): The brain adapts to using **ketone bodies** (acetoacetate and β-hydroxybutyrate) derived from free fatty acid oxidation. This reduces the requirement for gluconeogenesis, decreasing skeletal muscle proteolysis (reducing nitrogen loss from 10–12 g/day to 2–4 g/day) and lowering the Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) to conserve energy.
  • Stress Metabolism (Trauma, Sepsis, Major Surgery):
    • Driven by inflammatory cytokines (**TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6**) and counter-regulatory hormones (**Cortisol, Catecholamines, Glucagon**).
    • BMR increases (hypermetabolism) and glycogen stores are rapidly depleted.
    • High counter-regulatory hormones cause insulin resistance and block the shift to ketosis.
    • Muscle proteolysis occurs continuously to provide amino acids for hepatic acute-phase protein synthesis and gluconeogenesis, leading to rapid loss of lean body mass.
1.2

Cellular Mechanisms of Refeeding Syndrome

Refeeding syndrome is a life-threatening metabolic emergency caused by rapid nutritional replenishment in severely malnourished patients:

  • Baseline Malnourished State: Chronic starvation depletes intracellular electrolytes (phosphate, potassium, magnesium), though serum levels may remain normal due to contraction of extracellular volume. Intracellular ATP stores are also depleted.
  • Insulin-Mediated Shift: Reintroducing carbohydrates (dextrose) triggers a rapid release of **insulin** and stops glucagon secretion. Insulin activates the **Na+/K+ ATPase pump** (which co-transports phosphate, potassium, and magnesium into cells along with glucose).
  • Serum Depletion: This shift causes a sudden drop in serum levels of **phosphate, potassium, and magnesium**.
    • **Phosphate Depletion:** Limits the synthesis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG). The lack of ATP causes cellular dysfunction, presenting as diaphragmatic weakness (respiratory failure), cardiac arrhythmias, seizures, and red blood cell lysis.
1.3

Overfeeding Kinetics & Respiratory Quotient (RQ)

Overfeeding occurs when caloric intake exceeds energy expenditure, leading to lipogenesis and metabolic stress:

  • Respiratory Quotient Definition: The ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed: \[RQ = \frac{\text{VCO}_2}{\text{VO}_2}\]
  • Substrate Oxidation RQs:
    • Pure lipid oxidation: **0.7**
    • Pure protein oxidation: **0.8**
    • Pure carbohydrate oxidation: **1.0**
    • Lipogenesis (excess carbohydrate/overfeeding): **1.1 – 1.3**
  • Clinical Consequences: Overfeeding carbohydrates drives lipogenesis, producing excess carbon dioxide (\(\text{VCO}_2\)). This increases the work of breathing, which can trigger hypercapnic respiratory failure or make it difficult to wean patients from mechanical ventilation.

2.1

Visceral Protein Assessment Matrix

Serum proteins reflect hepatic synthesis rates but are confounded by the systemic inflammatory response:

Visceral Protein Serum Half-Life Normal Range Clinical Utility & Diagnostic Confounders
Albumin **20 Days** 3.5 – 5.0 g/dL Reflects long-term nutritional status. **Decreased by acute inflammation** (negative acute-phase reactant), fluid overload, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, and protein-losing enteropathy. Not useful for tracking acute nutritional changes.
Transferrin **8 – 10 Days** 200 – 400 mg/dL Reflects intermediate nutritional status. Decreased by acute inflammation. **Increased in iron deficiency states** (making it appear falsely normal in malnourished patients with concurrent iron deficiency).
Prealbumin (Transthyretin) **2 Days** 15 – 35 mg/dL **Best marker for tracking acute changes** and response to nutritional therapy. Decreased by acute inflammation and hepatic failure. **Falsely elevated in renal failure** due to decreased clearance.

PILLAR 3 Landmark Clinical Trials
3.1

Clinical Nutrition Trial Evidence

Trial Name Year Primary Intervention Clinical Outcome & Impact on Practice Guidelines
CALORIES 2014 Multicenter randomized trial comparing early enteral nutrition (EN) vs. early parenteral nutrition (PN) initiated within 36 hours of ICU admission in 2,400 patients. **No difference in 30-day mortality** (22.4% vs. 21.5%, p=0.57) or infectious complications between EN and PN. Challenged the historical assumption that parenteral nutrition is inherently associated with higher infection rates when glycemic control is maintained.
NUTRIREA-2 2018 Multicenter randomized trial comparing early EN vs. early PN in 2,410 critically ill patients with shock requiring high-dose vasopressor support. **No difference in 28-day mortality**, but the **early EN group had significantly higher rates of gastrointestinal complications**, including vomiting, diarrhea, and **acute bowel ischemia** (1.0% vs. 0.0%, p=0.007). Established that enteral nutrition should be delayed or initiated at a low trophic rate in patients with unstable hemodynamics or requiring high-dose vasopressors.
PILLAR 4 Surgical Strategy & Feeding Protocols
4.1

Refeeding Prevention & Titration Protocol

A standardized protocol is used to initiate nutrition safely in severely malnourished patients (e.g., BMI < 16, or no oral intake for > 7–10 days):

Malnutrition Reintroduction Protocol

  1. Electrolyte Correction: Measure and correct serum potassium, magnesium, and phosphate levels **before initiating feeding**.
  2. Thiamine Pre-treatment: Administer **Thiamine 100–300 mg IV** at least 30 minutes before feeding, and continue daily for 3–5 days to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy.
  3. Conservative Caloric Initiation: Start feeding at **10–15 kcal/kg/day** (typically 50% of the calculated target). Limit initial dextrose load to **100–150 g/day**.
  4. Fluid Restriction: Limit initial fluids to avoid volume overload, as insulin-induced antidiuresis can precipitate acute heart failure.
  5. Frequent Monitoring: Measure serum phosphate, potassium, and magnesium every **6–8 hours** for the first 48 hours, and daily thereafter.
  6. Slow Escalation: Gradually increase caloric intake by 20–25% every 24–48 hours to the target goal if electrolytes remain stable and corrected.
PILLAR 5 Crisis Care & Clinical Management
5.1

TPN Hypoglycemic Crash Protocol

Sudden cessation of high-dextrose TPN (e.g., due to line displacement or running out of TPN bags) causes rebound hypoglycemia due to high circulating insulin levels from pancreatic islet cell hyperplasia:

Immediate Bedside Action

1. Check blood glucose immediately.

2. **Hang 10% Dextrose in Water (D10W) IV** to run at the same rate as the TPN infusion.

3. Monitor blood glucose every 15–30 minutes until stable.

4. Do not administer bolus doses of D50W unless symptomatic, as this can trigger another round of insulin release and worsening rebound hypoglycemia.

5.2

Central Line Air Embolism Management

ABSITE Exam Trap: Central Line Air Embolism

Dislodgement or opening of a central venous catheter can allow air into the venous circulation, leading to right ventricular outflow tract obstruction (air lock). This presents as sudden dyspnea, hypoxia, chest pain, hypotension, and a classic **"mill-wheel" murmur**.

Bedside Rescue Protocol:
• Place the patient in **left lateral decubitus head-down (Durant's maneuver / Trendelenburg)** position. This traps the air bubble in the apex of the right ventricle, keeping the outflow tract clear.
• Administer 100% high-flow oxygen to help resorb the nitrogen in the bubble.
• Attempt to aspirate the air bubble through the distal port of the central venous catheter.

VIVA SCENARIO — POSTOPERATIVE REFRACTORY VENTILATOR WEAN
A 68-year-old male is in the ICU on POD 10 after a pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure). He is receiving TPN (delivering 35 kcal/kg/day, with 70% of calories from dextrose). The ICU team is unable to wean him from mechanical ventilation due to persistent hypercapnia (PaCO2 56 mmHg) and high minute ventilation. What is your diagnosis, and how do you manage this?
  • Diagnosis: Hypercapnic respiratory failure secondary to overfeeding and carbohydrate-induced lipogenesis (expected RQ > 1.0).
  • Management Plan:
    • Obtain a metabolic cart measurement (indirect calorimetry) to determine the patient's actual energy expenditure.
    • Reduce total caloric intake to target **20–25 kcal/kg/day** (avoiding overfeeding).
    • Adjust the TPN formulation to decrease the dextrose load and increase the lipid fraction, targeting a balanced respiratory quotient (RQ ~0.8–0.85).
  • Verification: Monitor arterial blood gases over the subsequent 24–48 hours to confirm resolution of hypercapnia before attempting to extubate.
