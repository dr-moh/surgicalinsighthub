PILLAR 1 Core Pathophysiology & Stress Physiology
1.1

HPA-Axis Suppression & Glucocorticoid Receptor Kinetics

Exogenous steroid administration suppresses the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis via negative feedback loop kinetics. Circulating glucocorticoids bind to glucocorticoid receptors in the hypothalamus, decreasing Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH) synthesis, and in the anterior pituitary, reducing Adrenocorticotropic Hormone (ACTH) expression. This leads to bilateral adrenal cortical atrophy:

  • Surgical Stress Vulnerability: Under physiological stress (e.g., surgical incision), cortisol secretion normally increases up to 5–10 fold to maintain cardiovascular tone, expand vascular reactivity to catecholamines (upregulation of vascular $\alpha_1$-adrenergic receptors), and modulate inflammatory responses. Patients with HPA axis suppression cannot mount this cortisol response, putting them at high risk for refractory vasodilatory shock and cardiovascular collapse (adrenal crisis).
  • Steroid Equivalencies & Physiological Baselines:
    • Normal baseline daily cortisol production is **15–20 mg/day** (equivalent to 5 mg of prednisone or 20 mg of hydrocortisone).
    • Suppression can occur with any dose equivalent to ≥ 20 mg/day hydrocortisone (or ≥ 5 mg/day prednisone) taken for ≥ 3 weeks.
1.2

Cardiovascular Emergence & Arteriole Wall Stress

Surgical emergence causes a surge in endogenous catecholamines, leading to tachycardia and hypertension. In patients with chronic uncontrolled hypertension, arteriole walls are thickened and stiffened by hyaline and hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis:

  • Left Ventricular Mechanics: According to Laplace’s Law: \[T = \frac{P \cdot r}{2h}\] where \(T\) is wall tension, \(P\) is transmural pressure, \(r\) is ventricular chamber radius, and \(h\) is wall thickness. A sudden rise in systemic vascular resistance (SVR) increases afterload and ventricular pressure, causing a rapid spike in myocardial wall tension.
  • Myocardial Ischemia & Decompensation: This spike in wall tension increases myocardial oxygen demand. Stiffened arteriole walls restrict oxygen delivery, which can trigger subendocardial ischemia, acute diastolic heart failure, intracranial hemorrhage, or arterial dissection.
1.3

Pulmonary Decompensation & Atelectasis

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) are primarily driven by physiological and mechanical changes:

  • Postoperative Atelectasis: Diaphragmatic dysfunction occurs due to phrenic nerve inhibition from abdominal manipulation and splinting from incisional pain. This, combined with surfactant loss, leads to alveolar collapse in dependent lung zones, reducing Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) by up to 50%. This causes ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch and intrapulmonary shunt.
  • Ventilatory Muscle Fatigue: Anesthetics and narcotics blunt the central respiratory drive, forcing respiratory muscles to work against stiff, collapsed alveoli. This can progress to hypercapnic respiratory failure if not managed with early mobilization and lung expansion techniques.

2.1

Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI / Lee's Score)

The RCRI is the gold standard for predicting perioperative Major Adverse Cardiac Events (MACE), including myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, complete heart block, and cardiac death:

**Score 1 (Class II):** **0.9%** MACE Risk

2.2

Caprini VTE Risk Assessment Matrix

The Caprini Score stratifies venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk to guide perioperative prophylaxis:

Caprini Score Staging VTE Risk Category Prophylaxis Recommendations & Chemical Prophylaxis Mandates
0 – 1 Points Very Low Risk Early, frequent ambulation. No chemical prophylaxis required.
2 Points Low Risk Mechanical prophylaxis only (Sequential Compression Devices [SCDs] or TED stockings).
3 – 4 Points Moderate Risk **Chemical prophylaxis** (LMWH, e.g., Enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily; or UFH 5,000 U SC q12h) PLUS mechanical prophylaxis.
≥ 5 Points High Risk **Chemical prophylaxis** (LMWH, e.g., Enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily; or UFH 5,000 U SC q8h) PLUS mechanical prophylaxis. Consider extended prophylaxis (4 weeks) for major oncologic pelvic/abdominal surgery.
PILLAR 3 Optimization Protocols & Trials
3.1

Perioperative Glycemic Titration Rules

Preoperative management of diabetic medications is required to avoid hyperglycemia (which impairs leukocyte function and increases wound infection risk) while preventing hypoglycemia under NPO conditions:

  • Oral & Non-Insulin Injectable Agents:
    • Metformin: Hold **24 hours prior to surgery** to minimize the risk of Metformin-Associated Lactic Acidosis (MALA), especially if renal perfusion falls intraoperatively.
    • SGLT-2 Inhibitors (e.g., Empagliflozin): Hold **3–4 days prior to surgery** to prevent euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (eugDKA).
    • GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (e.g., Semaglutide): Hold **1 week prior to elective surgery** to minimize gastric stasis and the risk of aspiration during induction.
    • Sulfonylureas (e.g., Glipizide): Hold the morning of surgery.
  • Exogenous Insulin Regimen:
    • Night Before Surgery: Administer **75–80%** of the standard long-acting basal dose (e.g., Glargine). Hold short-acting bolus insulin.
    • Morning of Surgery: Administer **50–75%** of the standard long-acting basal dose. Hold all short-acting doses unless blood glucose exceeds 180 mg/dL, in which case correct using a low-dose sliding scale.
3.2

Landmark Preoperative Optimization Trials

Trial Name Year Primary Intervention Clinical Outcome & Impact on Practice Guidelines
POISE-1 2008 Randomized trial comparing high-dose Metoprolol Succinate (100 mg starting 2–4 hours pre-op) vs. placebo in 8,351 patients. **Metoprolol reduced MI** (5.8% vs. 6.9%) but **significantly increased overall mortality** (3.1% vs. 2.3%) and **stroke** (1.0% vs. 0.5%), driven by hypotension. Established that **beta-blockers should not be initiated on the day of surgery**. Continue chronic therapy, but do not start immediately pre-op.
POISE-2 2014 Randomized trial evaluating perioperative Aspirin (200 mg starting pre-op) vs. placebo in 10,010 patients undergoing noncardiac surgery. **Aspirin did not reduce myocardial infarction or mortality** at 30 days (7.0% vs. 7.1%), but **significantly increased major bleeding events** (4.6% vs. 3.8%). Guided recommendations to hold prophylactic aspirin pre-op unless recent coronary stenting is present.
CARP 2004 Randomized trial comparing prophylactic coronary revascularization (CABG or PCI) vs. medical optimization in 510 patients with stable coronary artery disease undergoing major vascular surgery. **No difference in long-term mortality** (22% vs. 23% at 2.7 years) or postoperative MI. Proved that routine coronary revascularization before high-risk noncardiac surgery is not beneficial. Reserve revascularization for patients who meet standard, non-surgical indications.
PILLAR 4 Dual Management Pathways
4.1

Uncontrolled Hypertension Pathway

Preoperative management of severe hypertension (systolic BP ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥ 110 mmHg) depends on the surgical timeline:

Elective Surgical Track

• **Delay the surgery.**
• Do not use rapid IV antihypertensives (e.g., hydralazine/labetalol) in the holding area to "force" a normal blood pressure, as this can cause unpredictable cerebral or coronary hypoperfusion.
• Refer the patient to their primary care provider for oral medication adjustment and optimization over 2–4 weeks.

Emergency/Urgent Surgical Track

• **Proceed to the operating room.**
• Manage blood pressure intraoperatively using short-acting, titratable intravenous infusions to prevent rapid pressure fluctuations.
• **First-line agents:** **Clevidipine** (calcium channel blocker, quick onset and offset), **Nicardipine**, or **Esmolol** (short-acting β-blocker for tachycardia).

4.2

Severe Hyperglycemia Pathway

Preoperative management of severe hyperglycemia (blood glucose > 300 mg/dL) depends on the surgical timeline:

Elective Surgical Track

• **Delay the surgery.**
• Assess HbA1c. If HbA1c is > 8.5% (especially if > 10%), postpone elective cases to optimize glycemic control.
• Assess for acute ketosis. If positive, refer for medical management.

Emergency/Urgent Surgical Track

• **Proceed to the operating room.**
• Initiate a continuous **intravenous regular insulin infusion** (drip) rather than subcutaneous boluses, targeting a blood glucose range of **140–180 mg/dL**.
• Check arterial blood gas and basic metabolic panel to rule out Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) or Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS).

PILLAR 5 Crisis Care & Endocrine Management
5.1

Acute Adrenal Crisis Protocol

Suspected perioperative adrenal crisis is a life-threatening endocrine emergency presenting as refractory hypotension despite fluid resuscitation and vasopressors, often accompanied by hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia:

Immediate Bedside Crisis Protocol

  1. Blood Draw: Draw baseline serum cortisol and ACTH levels immediately. **Do not delay treatment while awaiting laboratory results.**
  2. Administer Stress-Dose Steroids:
    • **If an ACTH stimulation test is planned:** Administer **Dexamethasone 4 mg IV** immediately. Dexamethasone does not cross-react with serum cortisol assays, allowing the test to be performed.
    • **If no test is planned:** Administer **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV** immediately, followed by 50 mg IV every 8 hours.
  3. Fluid Resuscitation: Infuse **0.9% Normal Saline** or D5NS (if hypoglycemic) aggressively to correct hypovolemia and hyponatremia.
  4. Supportive Care: Monitor electrolytes closely; avoid potassium-sparing fluids, as hyperkalemia is often present due to mineralocorticoid deficiency.
5.2

Pheochromocytoma Preoperative Preparation

ABSITE Exam Trap: Pheochromocytoma Blockade Sequence

When preparing a patient with a pheochromocytoma for adrenalectomy, **alpha-blockade must always be initiated first** (using Phenoxybenzamine or Doxazosin) for 7–14 days. Beta-blockade (e.g., Metoprolol) is only started after alpha-blockade is established, typically 2–3 days before surgery, to control tachycardia.

*If a beta-blocker is started first, the loss of beta-2 mediated vasodilation leads to unopposed alpha-1 vasoconstriction by circulating catecholamines, causing a life-threatening hypertensive crisis.*

VIVA SCENARIO — ADRENAL INSUFFICIENCY COLLAPSE
A 54-year-old female with a history of severe rheumatoid arthritis managed with chronic prednisone (10 mg daily) undergoes an elective low anterior resection. In the PACU, her blood pressure drops to 72/38 mmHg. She is unresponsive to two 1-liter crystalloid boluses and a low-dose Norepinephrine infusion. What is your diagnosis, and what steps do you take?
  • Diagnosis: Acute Adrenal Crisis caused by HPA-axis suppression from chronic exogenous steroid use, unmasked by major surgical stress.
  • Initial Management:
    • Draw blood for baseline serum cortisol and ACTH levels immediately.
    • Administer **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV** bolus immediately. Do not wait for lab results.
    • Infuse **0.9% Normal Saline** rapidly.
  • Subsequent Management: Continue Hydrocortisone 50 mg IV every 8 hours, transitioning to her oral home dose once stable and tolerating oral intake.
