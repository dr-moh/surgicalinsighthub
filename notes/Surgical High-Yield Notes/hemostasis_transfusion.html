PILLAR 1 Core Pathophysiology & Cellular Mechanics
1.1

Cell-Based Model of Coagulation

The traditional "Y-shaped" cascade model has been replaced by the cell-based model of coagulation, which divides physiological hemostasis into three overlapping phases occurring on cellular surfaces:

  • Initiation (On Tissue Factor-bearing cells): Disrupted endothelium exposes subendothelial tissue factor (TF), which binds circulating Factor VIIa. The resulting **TF-VIIa complex** activates Factor IX and Factor X. Activated Factor X (Xa) associates with Factor Va on the TF-bearing cell surface to form the prothrombinase complex, converting a trace amount of prothrombin (Factor II) into thrombin (Factor IIa).
  • Amplification (On platelet surfaces): This trace thrombin activates nearby platelets, inducing shape change and degranulation. Thrombin activates Factors V, VIII, and XI on the platelet surface, and releases Factor VIII from its chaperone protein, Von Willebrand Factor (vWF).
  • Propagation (On platelet surfaces): Active Factor IXa pairs with its cofactor VIIIa on the platelet membrane, forming the **intrinsic tenase complex** to activate Factor X. Factor Xa then pairs with Factor Va, forming the **prothrombinase complex**, which generates a massive "thrombin burst." Thrombin rapidly converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin monomers, which are subsequently cross-linked and stabilized by Factor XIIIa.
1.2

The Biochemical "Lesion of Storage" in Banked Packed Red Blood Cells

As packed red blood cells (PRBCs) age in cold storage (1–6°C), they undergo progressive biochemical degradation that alters clinical performance:

  • 2,3-DPG Depletion: Levels of 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) fall. 2,3-DPG is required to stabilize the T-state (deoxygenated) of hemoglobin. Its loss **shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left**, increasing oxygen affinity and reducing the release of oxygen to peripheral tissues.
  • Supernatant Hyperkalemia: Cold temperatures inhibit the ATP-dependent Na+/K+-ATPase pump. Intracellular potassium leaks out of erythrocytes into the supernatant plasma, which can cause high serum potassium levels when transfused at high volumes.
  • ATP Depletion & Spherocytosis: Loss of ATP causes cell membrane remodeling and microvesicle shedding, transforming flexible biconcave cells into rigid **spherocytes**. These rigid cells are rapidly cleared by the recipient's splenic macrophages, decreasing post-transfusion survival.
  • Acidosis: Ongoing anaerobic glycolysis by stored erythrocytes generates lactic acid, lowering the unit pH to < 6.5.
1.3

Pathogenesis of Coagulopathies: HIT, vWD, and DIC

  • Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) Type II: An autoimmune condition that develops 5–10 days after heparin exposure. IgG antibodies form against **Platelet Factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complexes**. These IgG-PF4-heparin complexes bind to platelet **FcγRIIa receptors**, triggering platelet activation, degranulation, and microvascular thrombosis. This leads to thrombocytopenia due to consumption and a severe, prothrombotic state.
  • Von Willebrand Disease (vWD): The most common inherited bleeding disorder, presenting as:
    • Type 1 (Quantitative Deficiency): Mild-to-moderate reduction in normal vWF. Managed with **Desmopressin (DDAVP)**, which stimulates vWF release from endothelial Weibel-Palade bodies.
    • Type 2 (Qualitative Defect): Normal levels of dysfunctional vWF. Includes subtype **Type 2B**, where increased binding affinity causes spontaneous platelet aggregation and thrombocytopenia. DDAVP is contraindicated in Type 2B as it can worsen thrombocytopenia.
    • Type 3 (Severe Deficiency): Complete absence of vWF and low Factor VIII. Requires replacement with **vWF/Factor VIII concentrates**.
  • Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): Characterized by systemic tissue factor exposure from trauma or sepsis, leading to widespread thrombin generation. This causes microvascular fibrin thrombosis, organ ischemia, and consumption of platelets and clotting factors, resulting in concurrent hemorrhage.
PILLAR 2 Component Metrics & Diagnostic Scoring
2.1

Blood Component Payloads & Expected Yields

Intravenous blood component therapy must target specific volume and clinical endpoints:

Blood Component Volume Core Composition Clinical Payload / Expected Yield Clinical Indications
Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBC) ~250 – 350 mL Erythrocytes, trace plasma, anticoagulant solution **Increases Hb by 1 g/dL** and Hct by 3% in a standard adult. Symptomatic anemia, active hemorrhage, Hb < 7 g/dL.
Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) ~200 – 250 mL All coagulation factors, fibrinogen, albumin, antithrombin **Raises factor activity by 3–5%** per unit in standard adults. Coagulation factor deficiencies, microvascular bleeding, MTP.
Platelets (Apheresis) ~200 – 300 mL Concentrated platelets suspended in plasma **Increases platelet count by 30,000 – 50,000 / µL** per single unit. Thrombocytopenia < 10k/µL (prophylaxis) or < 50k/µL (pre-op), active bleeding.
Cryoprecipitate ~15 – 20 mL per bag (often pooled as 10 bags) Fibrinogen (~250 mg), vWF, Factor VIII, Factor XIII, fibronectin **Increases fibrinogen by 50 mg/dL** per 10-bag pool. Hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen < 150 mg/dL), vWD, uremic platelet dysfunction.
2.2

The 4Ts Scoring Matrix for Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia

The 4Ts score determines the clinical probability of HIT Type II before laboratory testing:

4T Category 2 Points 1 Point 0 Points
Thrombocytopenia Platelet fall > 50% AND platelet nadir ≥ 20 × 109/L. Platelet fall 30–50% OR platelet nadir 10–19 × 109/L. Platelet fall < 30% OR platelet nadir < 10 × 109/L.
Timing (of platelet fall) Onset day 5–10; OR onset ≤ 1 day (if heparin exposed within past 30 days). Onset > day 10; OR onset ≤ day 1 (if heparin exposed within past 31–100 days). Platelet fall ≤ 4 days without recent heparin exposure.
Thrombosis (sequelae) New thrombosis; skin necrosis at injection site; or systemic reaction post-bolus. Progressive/recurrent thrombosis; erythematous skin lesions; suspected thrombosis. None.
Other causes (of platelet fall) No other clinical explanation for platelet fall is apparent. Possible alternative explanation is present. Definite alternative explanation is present (e.g., severe sepsis).

Clinical Interpretation:
• Score 0–3: Low probability (> 99% Negative Predictive Value). Continue heparin.
• Score 4–5 (Intermediate) or 6–8 (High): Immediately **stop all heparin products** and initiate a non-heparin anticoagulant (e.g., Argatroban) while awaiting ELISA and Serotonin Release Assays.

PILLAR 3 Resuscitative Strategy & Trials
3.1

Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP) Administration

Massive Transfusion is defined as the transfusion of ≥ 10 units of PRBCs within 24 hours, or ≥ 4 units within 1 hour. Immediate administration must mimic whole blood:

  • Component Ratio: Target a 1:1:1 ratio of PRBCs to FFP to Platelets.
  • Monitoring & Correction Goals:
    • Platelets > 50,000/µL (> 100,000/µL in neurotrauma).
    • Fibrinogen > 150 mg/dL.
    • Core body temperature > 35°C.
    • Arterial pH > 7.20.
    • Ionized Calcium > 1.1 mmol/L. Citrate preservative in FFP and PRBCs binds calcium; monitor and replace with Calcium Gluconate or Chloride.
3.2

Landmark Transfusion & Hemostasis Trials

Trial Name Year Primary Intervention Clinical Outcome & Impact on Practice Guidelines
TRICC 1999 Randomized trial comparing restrictive (transfusion trigger Hb < 7 g/dL) vs. liberal (trigger Hb < 10 g/dL) transfusion thresholds in 838 critically ill patients. **Overall 30-day mortality was equivalent or lower in the restrictive group** (22.2% vs. 28.1%, p=0.10). Restrictive strategy was superior in younger and less severely ill patients. Established the standard target Hb of **7.0–8.0 g/dL**.
PROPPR 2015 Randomized trial comparing 1:1:1 vs. 1:1:2 (PRBC:FFP:Platelets) transfusion ratios in 680 severely injured bleeding trauma patients. No significant difference in overall 24-hour or 30-day mortality. However, the **1:1:1 group achieved significantly better hemostasis (86% vs 73%) and experienced fewer deaths due to exsanguination at 24 hours (9.2% vs 14.6%)**. Validate early high-ratio protocol.
PROMMTT 2013 Prospective observational study evaluating the timing of plasma and platelet administration in 905 trauma patients. Found that **transfusing higher ratios of FFP and platelets early during resuscitation (within the first 6 hours) was associated with significantly decreased mortality** at 24 hours. Led to the design of the PROPPR RCT.
PILLAR 4 Anticoagulation Reversal & Bridging
4.1

Anticoagulant Reversal Algorithms

Emergency reversal strategies for patients presenting with active hemorrhage or requiring urgent surgery are drug-specific:

  • Warfarin Reversal:
    • **Emergency (Active Major Bleeding):** Administer **4-Factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC)** (containing factors II, VII, IX, and X, along with Protein C and S) at 25–50 units/kg based on the baseline INR, plus **10 mg IV Vitamin K** (slow infusion to prevent anaphylaxis). FFP is second-line due to volume constraints and delay in preparation.
  • Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) Reversal:
    • Dabigatran (Direct Thrombin Inhibitor): Reverse with **Idarucizumab (Praxbind)**, a humanized monoclonal antibody Fab fragment that binds dabigatran with 350-fold higher affinity than thrombin. Dose: 5g IV (given as two 2.5g vials). Hemodialysis is a secondary option.
    • Apixaban & Rivaroxaban (Factor Xa Inhibitors): Reverse with **Andexanet Alfa (Andexxa)**, a recombinant decoy Factor Xa protein that binds and sequesters the inhibitor. Dose is determined by the DOAC dose and timing. Alternatively, use high-dose 4-Factor PCC (50 units/kg) if Andexanet is unavailable.
4.2

Perioperative Bridging Timelines

In elective surgical patients, anticoagulants must be managed to minimize the risk of bleeding while preventing thromboembolism:

  • Warfarin: Stop **5 days prior to surgery**. Bridge with therapeutic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH, e.g., Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg every 12 hours) in patients at high risk of thromboembolism (e.g., mechanical mitral valve, atrial fibrillation with CHADS2 ≥ 5, or recent VTE < 3 months). Stop LMWH **24 hours prior to incision**. Restart LMWH and Warfarin 12–24 hours postoperatively once hemostasis is secure.
  • DOACs: Stop **48 hours prior to high-bleeding-risk surgery** (or 24 hours prior to low-risk procedures). Stop **72 hours prior** in patients with a creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min who are taking dabigatran. Bridging is typically not required due to the rapid onset and offset of these agents.
PILLAR 5 Crisis Care & Transfusion Complications
5.1

Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction Bedside Protocol

Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR) is typically caused by ABO incompatibility. This leads to intravascular hemolysis, releasing free hemoglobin that can cause acute kidney injury (AKI) and DIC:

Immediate Bedside Action Plan (AHTR)

  1. Stop the Transfusion: Immediately disconnect the transfusion set from the IV hub. **Do not flush the remaining blood in the line.**
  2. Maintain IV Access: Connect a new IV tubing set and initiate a slow infusion of **0.9% Normal Saline** to maintain access.
  3. Clerical Verification: Re-check the patient ID band against the blood bag tag.
  4. Return Blood/Sample: Return the remaining blood product bag, tubing, and newly drawn patient blood and urine samples to the blood bank for direct Coombs testing.
  5. Prevent Renal Injury: Initiate aggressive IV hydration to maintain a urine output of **100–200 mL/hour** using Normal Saline. Consider alkalinization of urine to prevent precipitation of acid hematin in the renal tubules.
5.2

TRALI vs. TACO Clinical Differential

Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI) and Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO) present with acute respiratory distress, but require different management:

Clinical Parameter TRALI (Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury) TACO (Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload)
Pathophysiology Immune-mediated. Donor antibodies (HLA/HNA) activate recipient neutrophils in the pulmonary vasculature, causing endothelial injury and capillary leak. Hydrostatic overload. Excessive volume or rate of infusion exceeds cardiac capacity, leading to pulmonary congestion.
Cardiac Output Normal or Hyperdynamic (Non-cardiogenic) Decreased, Left Ventricular Strain (Cardiogenic)
Central Venous Pressure (CVP) / PAWP Normal or Low Elevated (CVP > 10 mmHg, PAWP > 18 mmHg)
Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) Normal **Significantly Elevated** (Post/Pre ratio > 1.5)
Vitals / Temperature **Fever** (Temp elevation > 1°C), **Hypotension** Normal Temp, **Hypertension** (widened pulse pressure)
Response to Diuretics Minimal to none (can worsen hypotension) **Excellent (therapeutic correction)**
BOARD REVIEW ABSITE Core Recall Matrix

High-Yield Recall Matrix

Board Question Topic Gold Standard Key Fact Surgical Decision / Clinical Action
TRICC Transfusion Target A restrictive transfusion strategy (trigger Hb < 7.0 g/dL) is safe and equivalent to a liberal target in critically ill patients, except those with active myocardial ischemia. Withhold transfusions in stable ICU patients unless Hb drops below 7.0 g/dL.
HIT Thromboembolic Risk IgG-PF4-heparin complexes activate platelets. Warfarin is contraindicated in acute HIT as it depletes Protein C and can cause venous limb gangrene. Initiate Argatroban. Do not start Warfarin until platelet count has recovered to > 150,000/µL.
vWD Type 2B Trap Desmopressin (DDAVP) is contraindicated in Type 2B vWD. It causes release of abnormal vWF, leading to platelet aggregation and severe thrombocytopenia. Avoid DDAVP in Type 2B; manage bleeding with Humate-P or intermediate-purity factor concentrates.
TRALI vs. TACO Management TACO is treated with aggressive loop diuretics. TRALI requires supportive mechanical ventilation; diuretics can worsen shock. Check post-transfusion BNP and vitals to differentiate before administering IV Furosemide.
VIVA SCENARIO — TRANSFUSION REACTION
A 45-year-old female is undergoing an elective ventral hernia repair. Ten minutes after starting a unit of packed red blood cells, the patient develops a fever of 38.9°C, hypotension (BP 82/40 mmHg), tachycardic arrhythmia, and oozing from her IV site. What is your diagnosis, and what steps do you take?
  • Diagnosis: Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR) due to ABO incompatibility, presenting with acute distributive shock and secondary DIC.
  • Action: Immediately stop the transfusion, disconnect the blood tubing, and attach new saline-filled tubing. Do not flush the line.
  • Management Steps:
    • Send the blood unit, tubing, and fresh patient blood/urine samples to the blood bank for Coombs testing and hemolysis assessment.
    • Initiate aggressive IV crystalloid resuscitation to maintain blood pressure and preserve renal perfusion (target urine output > 100 mL/hour).
    • Check coagulation parameters (PT, aPTT, Fibrinogen, Platelets) for DIC. Re-verify all patient and unit ID markers.
