CHAPTER 10

Classification & Etiopathogenesis of Hypothyroidism

10.1

Primary vs Central Framework & Diagnostic Biomarkers

Chapter Outline: Hypothyroidism Classification

  • Section 1. Introduction & Etiopathogenesis FrameworkDefinition of systemic thyroid hormone deficiency and primary vs central breakdown.
  • Section 2. Primary Hypothyroidism EtiologiesThyroid gland failure driven by Hashimoto's, I-131 ablation, thyroidectomy, or iodine deficiency.
  • Section 3. Central (Secondary & Tertiary) HypothyroidismPituitary TSH deficiency or hypothalamic TRH failure secondary to adenomas or trauma.
  • Section 4. Subclinical Hypothyroidism Definition & RiskElevated TSH with normal Free T4; clinical indications for levothyroxine initiation.
  • Section 5. Clinical Manifestations Across Organ SystemsLethargy, cold intolerance, weight gain, bradycardia, constipation, hypercholesterolemia.
  • Section 6. Laboratory Serologic Biomarker ProfilesInterpretation of TSH, Free T4, and autoantibody panels.
  • Section 7. Cardiovascular and Metabolic ImpactSystemic vascular resistance changes, pericardial effusion, and impaired cardiac contractility.
  • Section 8. Special Population Considerations (Elderly & Cardiac)Conservative start-low go-slow dosing strategies in patients with CAD.
  • Section 9. High-Yield Review & Hypothyroidism Board SummarySummary table of diagnostic laboratory profiles and treatment thresholds.

1. Introduction & Etiopathogenesis Framework

Hypothyroidism results from insufficient circulating thyroid hormone levels. Primary hypothyroidism accounts for 99% of cases and is caused by thyroid gland failure. Central (secondary/tertiary) hypothyroidism is caused by pituitary TSH deficiency or hypothalamic TRH deficiency.

2. Primary Hypothyroidism Etiologies

Primary etiologies include autoimmune Hashimoto's thyroiditis, post-surgical thyroidectomy, post-radioactive iodine ablation, and severe endemic iodine deficiency.

3. Central (Secondary & Tertiary) Hypothyroidism

Central hypothyroidism results from destructive pituitary macroadenomas, craniopharyngiomas, pituitary apoplexy, Sheehan syndrome, or cranial irradiation.

4. Subclinical Hypothyroidism Definition & Risk

Clinical Category Serum TSH Level Serum Free T4 Level Primary Etiologies & Action
Primary Hypothyroidism Elevated (>4.2 mIU/L) Low (<0.9 ng/dL) Hashimoto's, iatrogenic ablation. Treat with L-T4.
Central Hypothyroidism Low or inappropriately normal Low (<0.9 ng/dL) Pituitary macroadenoma, Sheehan syndrome. Evaluate adrenals.
Subclinical Hypothyroidism Elevated (4.5–10 mIU/L) Normal Treat if TSH >10, pregnancy, or positive TPOAb.

5. Clinical Manifestations Across Organ Systems

Clinical features include lethargy, cold intolerance, weight gain, bradycardia, constipation, dry skin, non-pitting periorbital edema (myxedema), and delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes.

6. Laboratory Serologic Biomarker Profiles

Primary disease presents with elevated TSH and low FT4. Central disease manifests low or inappropriately normal TSH alongside low FT4.

7. Cardiovascular and Metabolic Impact

Hypothyroidism increases systemic vascular resistance, decreases cardiac output, causes hypercholesterolemia (downregulated LDL receptors), and promotes pericardial effusion.

8. Special Population Considerations (Elderly & Cardiac)

In elderly patients or those with underlying coronary artery disease, initiate levothyroxine at low doses (12.5–25 mcg/day) to avoid precipitating myocardial ischemia.

9. High-Yield Review & Hypothyroidism Board Summary

  • Primary vs Central Hallmark: TSH is elevated in primary, low/normal in central.
  • Subclinical Treatment Threshold: TSH >10 mIU/L or positive TPOAb in pregnancy.
CHAPTER 11

Congenital Hypothyroidism & Dyshormonogenesis

11.1

Pediatric Screening & Metabolic Defects

Chapter Outline: Congenital Hypothyroidism

  • Section 1. Epidemiology and Newborn Screening Protocols1 in 3,000 live births; routine heel-stick TSH screening on day 2–4 of life.
  • Section 2. Thyroid Dysgenesis PathophysiologyThyroid aplasia, hypoplasia, or ectopy (lingual thyroid) accounting for 85% of cases.
  • Section 3. Dyshormonogenesis Enzyme DefectsAutosomal recessive enzymatic errors in iodide trapping, organification, or coupling (15%).
  • Section 4. Pendred Syndrome & Inborn ErrorsMutation in Pendrin gene (SLC26A4) causing sensorineural hearing loss and goiter.
  • Section 5. Clinical Signs in Newborns & InfantsMacroglossia, umbilical hernia, umbilical distension, prolonged jaundice, hypotonia, hoarse cry.
  • Section 6. Diagnostic Evaluation (Ultrasound & Scintigraphy)Tc-99m scanning distinguishing thyroid agenesis from ectopy or dyshormonogenesis.
  • Section 7. Emergency Pediatric Replacement DosingImmediate initiation of Levothyroxine (10–15 mcg/kg/day) to prevent cretinism.
  • Section 8. Neurocognitive Development OutcomesNormal intellectual development contingent on treatment initiation within 2 weeks of birth.
  • Section 9. High-Yield Pediatric Review & Board AnchorsCore pediatric recall points and enzymatic mutation profiles.

1. Epidemiology and Newborn Screening Protocols

Congenital hypothyroidism affects 1 in 3,000 live births. Mandatory newborn screening via heel-stick blood spot TSH on day 2–4 of life enables early detection.

2. Thyroid Dysgenesis Pathophysiology

Thyroid dysgenesis (ectopy 60%, aplasia 20%, hypoplasia 5%) accounts for 85% of congenital cases, stemming from developmental migration failure of the median thyroid primordium.

3. Dyshormonogenesis Enzyme Defects

Dyshormonogenesis (15% of cases) results from autosomal recessive defects in TPO organification, NIS trapping, thyroglobulin synthesis, or deiodination.

4. Pendred Syndrome & Inborn Errors

Pendred syndrome is caused by biallelic mutations in the SLC26A4 gene encoding Pendrin, characterized by congenital sensorineural hearing loss and euthyroid/hypothyroid goiter.

5. Clinical Signs in Newborns & Infants

Infants present with macroglossia, umbilical hernia, abdominal distension, prolonged unconjugated jaundice, hypothermia, hypotonia, and a hoarse cry (cretinism).

6. Diagnostic Evaluation (Ultrasound & Scintigraphy)

Technetium-99m or I-123 scintigraphy identifies ectopic thyroid tissue (e.g., lingual thyroid at the base of the tongue) vs complete agenesis.

7. Emergency Pediatric Replacement Dosing

Initiate oral Levothyroxine immediately at a high replacement dose of 10–15 mcg/kg/day upon screening confirmation.

8. Neurocognitive Development Outcomes

Starting L-T4 replacement within the first 2 weeks of life prevents irreversible neurodevelopmental impairment (cretinism) and secures normal IQ outcomes.

9. High-Yield Pediatric Review & Board Anchors

  • Most Common Cause: Thyroid dysgenesis / ectopic thyroid (85%).
  • Pendred Syndrome triad: Sensorineural deafness + Goiter + SLC26A4 mutation.
  • L-T4 Dosing: 10–15 mcg/kg/day within 2 weeks of life.
CHAPTER 12

Central Hypothyroidism & Pituitary Disorders

12.1

Pituitary Macroadenomas & TSH Deficiency

Chapter Outline: Central Hypothyroidism

  • Section 1. Definition and Etiology BreakdownSecondary pituitary TSH deficiency or tertiary hypothalamic TRH failure.
  • Section 2. Pituitary Adenomas and Mass EffectsNon-functioning pituitary macroadenomas causing compression of normal thyrotrope cells.
  • Section 3. Sheehan Syndrome & Vascular NecrosisPostpartum ischemic pituitary necrosis secondary to severe obstetric hemorrhage.
  • Section 4. Iatrogenic and Traumatic CausesCranial irradiation, neurosurgical resection, and traumatic brain injury (TBI).
  • Section 5. Clinical Evaluation & Combined Pituitary DeficitsCo-existing hypogonadism, GH deficiency, and secondary adrenal insufficiency.
  • Section 6. Diagnostic Workup & NeuroimagingLow/normal TSH with low FT4; MRI sella turcica defining mass lesions.
  • Section 7. Mandatory Adrenal Axis EvaluationBaseline morning cortisol and ACTH stimulation testing prior to L-T4 initiation.
  • Section 8. Therapeutic Dosing and Monitoring RulesL-T4 dosing monitored strictly via Free T4 levels (TSH cannot be used for surveillance).
  • Section 9. High-Yield Central Review & Board TrapsEssential board exam warnings regarding unreplaced adrenal insufficiency.

1. Definition and Etiology Breakdown

Central hypothyroidism stems from secondary pituitary TSH deficiency or tertiary hypothalamic TRH failure, accounting for <1% of hypothyroid cases.

2. Pituitary Adenomas and Mass Effects

Non-functioning pituitary macroadenomas (>10 mm) compress normal anterior pituitary tissue, causing sequential loss of GH, LH/FSH, TSH, and ACTH.

3. Sheehan Syndrome & Vascular Necrosis

Sheehan syndrome results from postpartum ischemic necrosis of the hypertrophied anterior pituitary gland triggered by severe obstetric hemorrhage and hypotension.

4. Iatrogenic and Traumatic Causes

Other causes include cranial radiotherapy for brain tumors, transsphenoidal neurosurgery, craniopharyngiomas, and severe traumatic brain injury.

5. Clinical Evaluation & Combined Pituitary Deficits

Patients manifest hypothyroid symptoms alongside amenorrhea/erectile dysfunction (hypogonadism), fatigue (adrenal insufficiency), and visual field cuts (bitemporal hemianopsia).

6. Diagnostic Workup & Neuroimaging

Laboratory testing demonstrates low Free T4 with a low or inappropriately normal TSH. Dedicated MRI of the sella turcica defines structural pituitary pathology.

7. Mandatory Adrenal Axis Evaluation

MANDATORY BOARD RULE — CORTICOSTEROID FIRST PROTOCOL In central hypothyroidism, you MUST evaluate and rule out secondary adrenal insufficiency (via morning cortisol or Cosyntropin stimulation test) prior to starting levothyroxine. Administering levothyroxine alone increases metabolic clearance of cortisol and can precipitate fatal adrenal crisis. Glucocorticoids MUST be started before L-T4.

8. Therapeutic Dosing and Monitoring Rules

Initiate L-T4 replacement. Monitoring is performed strictly using serial serum Free T4 levels (targeting mid-to-upper normal range); serum TSH is unmeasurable/unreliable for monitoring central disease.

9. High-Yield Central Review & Board Traps

  • Surveillance Biomarker: Monitor Free T4, NOT TSH.
  • Adrenal Rule: Always replace Cortisol before Levothyroxine.
CHAPTER 13

Levothyroxine Kinetics & Myxedema Resuscitation

13.1

L-T4 Dosing & Myxedema Coma Emergency Protocol

Chapter Outline: Levothyroxine Kinetics & Myxedema

  • Section 1. Pharmacokinetics of Synthetic Levothyroxine (L-T4)Half-life of 7 days, jejunal/ileal absorption kinetics, and steady-state timing (6–8 weeks).
  • Section 2. Standard Replacement Dosing StrategiesFull replacement dose calculation (1.6 mcg/kg/day based on lean body mass).
  • Section 3. Gastrointestinal Absorption InterferencesImpairment by calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, PPIs, sucralfate, bile acid sequestrants, and celiac disease.
  • Section 4. Drug Interactions Increasing L-T4 ClearanceHepatic CYP3A4 induction by Rifampin, Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, and Phenobarbital.
  • Section 5. Therapeutic Surveillance & Dose TitrationAdjusting doses in 12.5–25 mcg increments every 6–8 weeks targeting euthyroid TSH.
  • Section 6. Myxedema Coma Definition and Precipitating FactorsLife-threatening decompensated hypothyroidism triggered by infection, cold exposure, surgery, or sedatives.
  • Section 7. Clinical Presentation of Myxedema ComaHypothermia (<35°C), severe bradycardia, hyponatremia, hypercapnia, hypoventilation, and coma.
  • Section 8. ICU Emergency Resuscitation ProtocolsHemodynamic support, passive rewarming, empirical IV hydrocortisone, and loading IV levothyroxine.
  • Section 9. High-Yield Review & Emergency Resuscitation SummaryConsolidated ICU resuscitation flowchart for board examinations.

1. Pharmacokinetics of Synthetic Levothyroxine (L-T4)

Synthetic L-T4 has a long circulating half-life of 7 days. Absorption occurs primarily in the jejunum and ileum under acidic gastric conditions. Steady-state serum levels are achieved 6–8 weeks after dose initiation or adjustment.

2. Standard Replacement Dosing Strategies

Full replacement dose for young healthy adults post-thyroidectomy is 1.6 mcg/kg/day based on ideal body mass. Elderly patients or those with CAD start at 12.5–25 mcg/day.

3. Gastrointestinal Absorption Interferences

Co-administration of calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, aluminum hydroxide, proton pump inhibitors, sucralfate, or cholestyramine significantly impairs GI absorption. L-T4 must be taken on an empty stomach with water 30–60 minutes before breakfast.

4. Drug Interactions Increasing L-T4 Clearance

Hepatic microsomal enzyme inducers (Rifampin, Phenytoin, Carbamazepine) accelerate T4 clearance, requiring higher daily replacement doses.

5. Therapeutic Surveillance & Dose Titration

Monitor serum TSH 6–8 weeks post-adjustment. Titrate L-T4 in 12.5–25 mcg increments until TSH normalizes (0.4–2.5 mIU/L).

6. Myxedema Coma Definition and Precipitating Factors

Myxedema coma is a life-threatening endocrine emergency representing severe decompensated hypothyroidism, precipitated by sepsis, cold exposure, trauma, stroke, or sedatives in long-standing untreated patients.

7. Clinical Presentation of Myxedema Coma

Features include profound hypothermia (<35°C), hypoventilation, hypercapnia, hyponatremia (SIADH), bradycardia, pericardial effusion, non-pitting edema, and altered mental status progressing to coma.

8. ICU Emergency Resuscitation Protocols

EMERGENCY RESUSCITATION — MYXEDEMA COMA PROTOCOL Myxedema coma requires immediate ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, passive rewarming (avoid active rewarming to prevent vasodilation/shock), and IV administration of:
  1. IV Hydrocortisone (100 mg q8h): MUST be administered PRIOR to levothyroxine to prevent precipitating acute adrenal crisis.
  2. IV Levothyroxine: Loading dose of 200–400 mcg IV push, followed by 50–100 mcg IV daily.

9. High-Yield Review & Emergency Resuscitation Summary

  • Full Replacement Dose: 1.6 mcg/kg/day.
  • Steady-State Timing: Recheck TSH in 6-8 weeks.
  • Myxedema Coma Order: IV Hydrocortisone FIRST, then IV Levothyroxine.
