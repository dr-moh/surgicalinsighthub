Surgical Infections, Antibiotics & Wound Management

Foundational Anchor (From PDF Source Notes)

"Rational use of antibiotics, mechanisms of antibiotic resistance, selection parameters, causes of treatment failure, surgical wound classification (Clean, Clean-Contaminated, Contaminated, Dirty) and SSI risk, tetanus-prone wound protocols, five pillars of infection control, and methods of sterilization vs. disinfection." Clinical Focus Note: Prophylactic antibiotic administration is a pharmacokinetic race to achieve minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) in the target tissue *before* the first blade-to-skin incision. Postoperative extension is a common driver of resistance.

1. Molecular Resistance Kinetics & Tissue-Destructive Infections

Genetics and Enzymes of Antimicrobial Resistance

MRSA (mecA Gene): Methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus is mediated by the acquisition of the mobile genetic element SCCmec carrying the mecA gene. This gene encodes Penicillin-Binding Protein 2a (PBP2a), a modified transpeptidase with an exceptionally low binding affinity for standard $\beta$-lactam rings. Cell wall peptidoglycan cross-linking continues uninhibited despite lethal concentrations of standard penicillins or cephalosporins.

VRE (vanA Alteration): Vancomycin resistance in Enterococcus faecium/faecalis is driven by operons (typically vanA/vanB) that structurally re-engineer cell wall synthesis components. The bacteria switch the terminal cell wall peptide sequence from D-Alanyl-D-Alanine (the high-affinity target for vancomycin binding) to D-Alanyl-D-Lactate (D-Ala-D-Lac). This simple ester modification decreases vancomycin binding affinity by a factor of 1,000.

ESBL (Plasmid Expansion): Extended-Spectrum $\beta$-Lactamases are plasmid-mediated enzymes (e.g., CTX-M, TEM, SHV) that cleave the structural cyclic amide bond of third-generation cephalosporins and monobactams. These plasmids frequently co-transmit resistance vectors for fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides, restricting therapy to carbapenems.

Pathogenesis of NSTIs & Clostridial Cascades

Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections (NSTIs): Classified microbiologically into two main divisions:

  • Type I (Polymicrobial): Synergy between obligate anaerobes (Bacteroides, Clostridium) and facultative anaerobes (Enterobacteriaceae, streptococci). Anaerobes create profound tissue hypoxia, which impairs neutrophil killing capacity and accelerates gas production.
  • Type II (Monomicrobial): Dominated by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep) or Staphylococcus aureus. S. pyogenes expresses surface M-protein which resists phagocytosis and releases potent exotoxins (SpeA, SpeC). These exotoxins act as **superantigens**, binding directly to MHC Class II complexes and T-cell receptors without processing. This triggers a massive, uncoordinated T-cell clonal explosion, driving rapid systemic septic shock.

Clostridium tetani Neurotransmission: Spores produce the neurotoxin tetanospasmin. The toxin binds irreversibly to gangliosides on peripheral nerve terminals and hitches a ride via retrograde axonal transport into the central nervous system. It directly cleaves synaptobrevin, a critical vesicle-associated membrane protein (VAMP) within inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw cells). This blocks the release of GABA and glycine, causing uninhibited $\alpha$-motor neuron firing, resulting in severe tetanic muscle spasms and trismus.

2. CDC Wound Classification & Physical Sterilization Parameters

CDC Wound Classification Operative Criteria & Anatomic Boundaries Expected Surgical Site Infection (SSI) Risk
Class I: Clean Uninfected operative wound where no inflammation is encountered. The respiratory, alimentary, genital, or uninfected urinary tracts are **not entered**. Primary mechanical closure without drainage is achieved.
Examples: Elective inguinal hernia repair, thyroidectomy, simple mastectomy. 		< 2.0%
Class II: Clean-Contaminated Operative wounds where the respiratory, alimentary, genital, or urinary tracts are entered under **controlled, supervised conditions** and without unusual contamination. No evidence of infection or major break in sterile technique.
Examples: Elective cholecystectomy, uncomplicated appendectomy, Whipple procedure. 		2.0% – 10.0%
Class III: Contaminated Open, fresh, accidental wounds. Operations with **major breaks in sterile technique** or gross spillage from the gastrointestinal tract. Incisions presenting acute, non-purulent inflammation are automatically grouped here.
Examples: Open trauma laparotomy for blunt bowel injury, enterocutaneous fistula resection with gross spillage. 		10.0% – 20.0%
Class IV: Dirty / Infected Old traumatic wounds with retained devitalized tissue; wounds that involve existing **clinical infection or perforated viscera**. This classification implies that the organisms causing post-op infection were present in the field *before* the operation.
Examples: Perforated diverticulitis with feculent peritonitis, debridement of ischemic gas gangrene. 		> 30.0%

Physical Parameters for Moist Heat (Steam) Autoclaving

Gravity Displacement Cycle
  • Minimum Temperature: 121°C (250°F)
  • Chamber Pressure: 15 psi
  • Minimum Time Constraints: **30 minutes** (for wrapped loads)
  • Mechanism: Steam is introduced at top/sides of chamber, pushing heavier cool air down and out the exhaust port.
Pre-vacuum (Dynamic-Air-Removal) Cycle
  • Minimum Temperature: 132°C (270°F)
  • Chamber Pressure: 27 – 30 psi
  • Minimum Time Constraints: **4 minutes** (standard cycle)
  • Mechanism: Active vacuum pump removes air before steam injection, permitting near-instantaneous steam penetration into dense packages.

3. Quality Metrics: SCIP Consensus & Tetanus Resuscitation

The **Surgical Care Improvement Project (SCIP)** established standardized protocol bundles to reduce surgical site infections, verified in national quality datasets:

SCIP Parameter Primary Quality Standard & Intervention Evidence-Based Clinical Rationale
Pre-Incision Timing Infusion must begin **within 60 minutes prior to surgical incision** (120 minutes for Vancomycin or Fluoroquinolones). Ensures tissue antibiotic concentration exceeds the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) at the exact moment of incision.
Intraoperative Re-dosing Re-dose if duration exceeds **two half-lives** of the agent (e.g., Cefazolin re-dosed at 4 hours) or blood loss exceeds **1,500 mL**. Maintains therapeutic serum and tissue drug levels during extended exposures or dilution from bleeding.
Discontinuation Rules Prophylactic antibiotics must be stopped **within 24 hours of anesthesia end-time** (48 hours for cardiac surgery). Prolonged postoperative administration does not reduce infection rates and strongly drives drug-resistant strains.
Glycemic Target Maintain perioperative blood glucose **≤ 180 mg/dL** in all surgical patients. Reduces hyperglycemia-induced leukocyte dysfunction, which impairs phagocytosis and bacterial intracellular killing.
Oxygenation & Temp Maintain core body temp **≥ 36.0°C** and optimize perioperative oxygenation (FiO2 ≥ 80% for intubated patients). Hypothermia causes cutaneous vasoconstriction, decreasing oxygen tension. High FiO2 enhances neutrophil oxidative burst activity.

4. Surgical Tactics & Source Control

Fournier's Gangrene Resuscitation & Debridement

Fournier's Gangrene is a necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum and scrotum, requiring rapid intervention:

  • Aggressive Resuscitation: Initiate large-volume crystalloid infusion to restore organ perfusion. Correct acidosis.
  • Toxin Suppression: Administer IV Clindamycin (suppresses SpeA/SpeC protein synthesis at 50S ribosome) alongside Vancomycin and Piperacillin/Tazobactam.
  • Emergency Surgical Debridement: Debride all necrotic skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia. Expose down to healthy, bleeding tissue.
  • Testicular Preservation: Testicular blood supply arises from the abdominal aorta via the testicular arteries, protecting them from superficial scrotal fascial gangrene. Do not perform orchiectomy unless direct parenchymal necrosis is confirmed.

The Open Abdomen Dressing Timing Pitfall

Warning: Negative Pressure Wound Therapy Duration

When managing an open abdomen (e.g., damage control surgery, severe peritonitis), a temporary closure dressing (negative pressure wound therapy) must be cycled frequently.

Leaving a temporary abdominal closure dressing unchanged for > 48 hours dramatically increases the risk of severe bowel wall edema, adhesion formation, and direct mechanical breakdown of the intestinal wall, leading to catastrophic enterocutaneous fistulas. Schedule re-exploration and washout every 24–48 hours until closure is feasible.

5. Sterilization vs. Disinfection & Clostridial Myonecrosis

High-Level Disinfection vs. True Sterilization

True Sterilization: The complete destruction of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial endospores (e.g., Bacillus and Clostridium). Required for **critical devices** that enter sterile tissues or the vascular system (e.g., implants, vascular catheters, surgical instruments).

High-Level Disinfection (HLD): Destroys all vegetative microorganisms, mycobacteria, fungi, and lipid/non-lipid viruses, but **does not guarantee the destruction of all bacterial endospores**. Acceptable for **semi-critical devices** that contact mucous membranes or non-intact skin (e.g., flexible endoscopes, laryngoscope blades).

Clostridial Myonecrosis (Gas Gangrene) Diagnostics

Usually caused by Clostridium perfringens, a Gram-positive anaerobic rod that releases **alpha-toxin** (lecithinase), causing cell lysis and gas production:

  • Clinical presentation: Sudden, severe pain out of proportion to exam, crepitus, dishwater drainage, and bronze skin discoloration.
  • Microbiology Trap: Gram stain of wound exudate reveals **large Gram-positive rods with a complete absence of white blood cells (PMNs)**. The PMNs are lysed in the local environment by alpha-toxin.
  • Treatment: Immediate surgical debridement, IV Penicillin G + Clindamycin, and hyperbaric oxygen.

VIVA SCENARIO — HIGH-RISK CONTAMINATED WOUND
A 42-year-old construction worker presents to the trauma bay with a deep puncture wound to his left calf from a rusty rebar that was buried in damp soil. His immunization history is unknown. How do you manage his surgical wound and tetanus risk?
  • Wound Management: Perform immediate bedside irrigation and debridement of any devitalized tissue. This is classified as a Class III (Contaminated) or Class IV (Dirty) wound. Leave the wound open to heal by secondary intention (or plan delayed primary closure in 3–5 days). Do not close primarily.
  • Tetanus Prophylaxis: Because the patient has an unknown vaccination history and the wound is dirty/soil-contaminated, he must receive:
    1. Tetanus-Diphtheria-Pertussis (Tdap) Vaccine: 0.5 mL IM to initiate a 3-dose series.
    2. Tetanus Immune Globulin (TIG): 250 units IM administered at a separate anatomical site to provide immediate passive immunity.
  • Antibiotics: Administer prophylactic antibiotics targeting skin flora and anaerobic organisms.
