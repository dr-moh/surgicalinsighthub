CHAPTER 23

Thyroid Physiology & Disease in Pregnancy

23.1

Gestational Physiology & Thionamide Protocols

Chapter Outline: Thyroid Disease in Pregnancy

  • Section 1. Gestational Maternal Physiology AlterationsElevated hCG alpha-subunit TSHR stimulation and estrogen-induced TBG elevation.
  • Section 2. Transient Gestational ThyrotoxicosisFirst trimester self-limiting hyperthyroidism secondary to peak hCG levels.
  • Section 3. Diagnostic Laboratory Trimester Reference RangesTrimester-specific TSH reference cutoffs and free hormone equilibrium dialysis.
  • Section 4. Gestational Graves' Disease ManagementMonitoring maternal TSI titers and assessing fetal tachycardia / goiter risk.
  • Section 5. Thionamide Selection Protocols (PTU vs Methimazole)First trimester PTU preference vs 2nd/3rd trimester Methimazole transition.
  • Section 6. Teratogenic Risk & Embryopathy BreakdownMethimazole embryopathy (aplasia cutis, choanal atresia) vs PTU black-box hepatotoxicity.
  • Section 7. Surgical Indications During PregnancyTiming of mandatory thyroid surgery during the 2nd trimester.
  • Section 8. Postpartum Maternal SurveillanceMonitoring for postpartum thyroiditis rebound recurrence post-delivery.
  • Section 9. High-Yield Review & Gestational Board SummarySummary of trimester thionamide selection rules and TSH cutoffs.

1. Gestational Maternal Physiology Alterations

Pregnancy alters maternal thyroid physiology due to elevated human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) sharing structural alpha-subunit homology with TSH, and high estrogen increasing serum Thyroxine-Binding Globulin (TBG).

2. Transient Gestational Thyrotoxicosis

Peak hCG levels during weeks 8–11 transiently stimulate TSH receptors, causing mild self-limiting thyrotoxicosis without autoimmune autoantibodies.

3. Diagnostic Laboratory Trimester Reference Ranges

Serum TSH reference ranges are lower in pregnancy (1st trimester 0.1–2.5 mIU/L; 2nd trimester 0.2–3.0 mIU/L; 3rd trimester 0.3–3.0 mIU/L).

4. Gestational Graves' Disease Management

Graves' disease during pregnancy requires careful monitoring of maternal TSI autoantibodies, which cross the placenta and can induce fetal hyperthyroidism and goiter.

5. Thionamide Selection Protocols (PTU vs Methimazole)

BOARD WARNING — THIONAMIDES IN PREGNANCY During the 1st trimester of pregnancy, Propylthiouracil (PTU) is preferred because Methimazole is associated with embryopathy (aplasia cutis and choanal atresia). Switch to Methimazole for the 2nd and 3rd trimesters due to PTU black-box risk of severe maternal hepatotoxicity.

6. Teratogenic Risk & Embryopathy Breakdown

Methimazole exposure during organogenesis (weeks 6–10) risks aplasia cutis congenita and congenital gastrointestinal atresia. PTU hepatotoxicity carries a FDA black-box warning.

7. Surgical Indications During Pregnancy

If surgery is required for medication intolerance or large compressive goiter, operation is safely performed during the 2nd trimester (weeks 14–24) to minimize teratogenic anesthesia risks (1st trimester) and preterm labor risks (3rd trimester).

8. Postpartum Maternal Surveillance

Evaluate maternal thyroid function 6 weeks and 3 months postpartum to detect immune rebound thyroiditis.

9. High-Yield Review & Gestational Board Summary

  • 1st Trimester Thionamide: PTU (prevents Methimazole embryopathy).
  • 2nd/3rd Trimester Thionamide: Methimazole (prevents PTU hepatotoxicity).
  • Surgical Timing: 2nd trimester (weeks 14–24).
CHAPTER 24

Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS / Euthyroid Sick)

24.1

Critical Care Deiodinase Alterations

Chapter Outline: Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS)

  • Section 1. Introduction and Clinical DefinitionAbnormal serum thyroid hormone profiles in severe non-thyroidal critical illness without intrinsic gland dysfunction.
  • Section 2. Pathophysiology & Tissue Deiodinase AlterationsSystemic cytokine-mediated inhibition of D1 5'-deiodination and activation of D3 inner-ring deiodination.
  • Section 3. Biochemical Stage Progression in Critical CareEarly stage low T3, progressing to severe stage low T3 and low T4 with suppressed TSH.
  • Section 4. Reverse T3 (rT3) Diagnostic Elevation KineticsDiagnostic accumulation of metabolically inactive rT3 in septic and trauma patients.
  • Section 5. Prognostic Correlation with MortalityDegree of T4 suppression correlating directly with ICU mortality (>80% mortality when T4 <2 mcg/dL).
  • Section 6. Differential Diagnosis vs Central HypothyroidismDistinguishing NTIS (high rT3, acute illness) from true central pituitary failure (low rT3).
  • Section 7. Consensus Treatment GuidelinesEvidence confirming thyroid hormone substitution (L-T4 or L-T3) is NOT beneficial and potentially harmful.
  • Section 8. Resolution Kinetics Post-RecoveryTransient TSH elevation into subclinical range during clinical ICU recovery.
  • Section 9. High-Yield Review & Critical Care Board SummarySummary of deiodinase shifts and consensus treatment non-intervention rules.

1. Introduction and Clinical Definition

Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome (NTIS, euthyroid sick syndrome) refers to characteristic alterations in circulating thyroid hormone levels occurring in critically ill ICU patients, trauma, major sepsis, or starvation, without intrinsic thyroid pathology.

2. Pathophysiology & Tissue Deiodinase Alterations

Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-alpha) inhibit hepatic Deiodinase type 1 (D1), reducing outer-ring conversion of T4 to active T3. Concurrently, Deiodinase type 3 (D3) is upregulated, converting T4 to inactive reverse T3 (rT3).

3. Biochemical Stage Progression in Critical Care

Early NTIS manifests isolated low serum T3 with normal T4 and TSH. Severe prolonged critical illness progresses to low T3, low T4, and transiently suppressed TSH.

4. Reverse T3 (rT3) Diagnostic Elevation Kinetics

Serum reverse T3 (rT3) is markedly elevated due to impaired clearance and active D3 synthesis, serving as the biochemical hallmark of NTIS.

5. Prognostic Correlation with Mortality

In critical illness, the severity of serum T4 suppression correlates inversely with survival; total T4 levels <2 mcg/dL predict an ICU mortality rate exceeding 80%.

6. Differential Diagnosis vs Central Hypothyroidism

Distinguish NTIS from central hypothyroidism by checking reverse T3: rT3 is elevated in NTIS, but reduced/low in true central pituitary failure.

7. Consensus Treatment Guidelines

CLINICAL PEARL — NTIS MANAGEMENT Thyroid hormone replacement is NOT recommended in Non-Thyroidal Illness Syndrome. Randomized controlled trials confirm L-T4 or L-T3 administration does not improve survival and may exacerbate tissue catabolism. The hormone shifts represent a protective metabolic adaptation reducing energy expenditure during severe stress.

8. Resolution Kinetics Post-Recovery

As critical illness resolves, serum TSH transiently surges into the mildly elevated subclinical range (5–10 mIU/L) before completely normalizing alongside T4 and T3.

9. High-Yield Review & Critical Care Board Summary

  • Biochemical Hallmark: Low T3 with elevated Reverse T3 (rT3).
  • Deiodinase Shift: Inactive D1, activated D3.
  • Treatment Consensus: Do NOT treat with Levothyroxine.

Pharmacological Influences: Amiodarone & Lithium

25.1

Amiodarone-Induced Thyrotoxicosis (AIT-1 vs AIT-2)

Chapter Outline: Amiodarone & Lithium Dysfunction

  • Section 2. Amiodarone-Induced Hypothyroidism (AIH)Wolff-Chaikoff blockade failure to escape in underlying Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
  • Section 3. Amiodarone-Induced Thyrotoxicosis (AIT) BreakdownClassification into AIT Type 1 (iodine-induced hyperthyroidism) vs AIT Type 2 (destructive thyroiditis).
  • Section 4. AIT Type 1 Pathophysiology & Doppler FlowJod-Basedow effect in underlying nodule/Graves showing marked hypervascularity on color flow Doppler.
  • Section 5. AIT Type 2 Pathophysiology & Doppler FlowDirect drug-induced toxic destructive thyroiditis showing avascular gland on color flow Doppler.
  • Section 6. Differential Diagnostic Workup (RAIU & Doppler)Comparing hypervascular AIT-1 vs avascular AIT-2 to dictate medical therapy.
  • Section 7. Medical Management Protocols (AIT-1 vs AIT-2)Thionamides + Potassium Perchlorate for AIT-1 vs high-dose oral Glucocorticoids for AIT-2.
  • Section 8. Lithium-Induced Thyroid DysfunctionInhibition of thyroid hormone release causing goiter (50%) and hypothyroidism (20%).
  • Section 9. High-Yield Review & Drug Dysfunction Board MatrixConsolidated differential diagnostic matrix for medical/surgical board exams.

1. Pharmacology of Amiodarone Iodine Content

Amiodarone contains 37% iodine by weight. A standard 200 mg daily dose releases 7 mg of free inorganic iodine into circulation—more than 45 times the recommended daily adult intake of 150 mcg.

2. Amiodarone-Induced Hypothyroidism (AIH)

Excess iodine induces temporary inhibition of hormone synthesis (Wolff-Chaikoff effect). Patients with underlying Hashimoto's fail to escape this blockade, developing persistent hypothyroidism treated with L-T4.

3. Amiodarone-Induced Thyrotoxicosis (AIT) Breakdown

Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis is divided into two distinct pathophysiologic entities requiring opposing medical treatments:

Section 4 & 5. AIT Type 1 vs AIT Type 2 Differentiation

Diagnostic Feature AIT Type 1 (Jod-Basedow Effect) AIT Type 2 (Destructive Thyroiditis)
Underlying Pathology Iodine-induced autonomous synthesis in underlying nodule or Graves. Direct drug-induced toxic chemical destructive thyroiditis in normal gland.
Color Flow Doppler Hypervascularity (increased parenchymal blood flow). Avascular (absent/decreased blood flow).
First-Line Therapy High-dose Thionamides (Methimazole) + Potassium Perchlorate. High-dose oral Glucocorticoids (Prednisone 40–60 mg/day).

6. Differential Diagnostic Workup (RAIU & Doppler)

Color flow Doppler ultrasound is the primary diagnostic tool. RAIU is typically low in both due to heavy systemic iodine saturation.

7. Medical Management Protocols (AIT-1 vs AIT-2)

AIT-1 requires antithyroid thionamides to block synthesis. AIT-2 responds rapidly to oral prednisone. In mixed or uncertain cases, initiate combined thionamide + corticosteroid therapy.

8. Lithium-Induced Thyroid Dysfunction

Lithium concentrates in the thyroid gland and inhibits thyroid hormone release. It causes goiter in 50% and hypothyroidism in 20% of long-term users. Treat with Levothyroxine while continuing lithium therapy.

9. High-Yield Review & Drug Dysfunction Board Matrix

  • AIT-1 Hallmark: Hypervascular on Doppler -> Treat with Thionamides.
  • AIT-2 Hallmark: Avascular on Doppler -> Treat with Prednisone.
  • Lithium Action: Inhibits thyroid hormone release.
