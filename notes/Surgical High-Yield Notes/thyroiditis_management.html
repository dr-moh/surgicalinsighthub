CHAPTER 7

Hashimoto's Autoimmune Thyroiditis

7.1

Autoimmune Destruction & Primary Lymphoma Risk

  • Section 8. Surgical Indications in Hashimoto's DiseaseCompressive goiter, suspected malignancy, or rapid enlargement evaluating lymphoma.

1. Introduction and Autoimmune Pathogenesis

Hashimoto's thyroiditis (chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis) is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions. It is characterized by cell-mediated and humoral autoimmune destruction of thyroid follicular cells.

2. Histopathology and Hürthle Cell Transformation

Histologically, Hashimoto's shows dense lymphocytic infiltrates with germinal center formation, follicular destruction, and eosinophilic Hürthle cells (Askanazy cells) featuring metaplastic mitochondria-rich cytoplasm.

3. Serologic Biomarkers (Anti-TPO & Anti-Tg)

Patients present with markedly elevated anti-TPO antibodies (>95%) and anti-Tg antibodies (80%), serving as diagnostic serologic hallmarks.

4. Clinical Presentation and Progression to Hypothyroidism

Parenchymal inflammation presents as a painless, diffuse, firm goiter. Early follicle lysis can cause transient hyperthyroidism ("hashitoxicosis"), followed by permanent hypothyroidism.

5. Ultrasonographic Features of Hashimoto's

Ultrasound reveals a diffusely heterogeneous, hypoechoic architecture traversed by fibrous bands and micronodular pseudonodules.

6. Medical Management & Levothyroxine Replacement

Standard therapy is lifelong oral Levothyroxine (L-T4) replacement tailored to restore serum TSH to normal reference ranges.

7. Primary Thyroid Lymphoma Risk

BOARD WARNING — PRIMARY THYROID LYMPHOMA Patients with long-standing Hashimoto's thyroiditis have a 60-fold increased risk of developing primary thyroid lymphoma (typically non-Hodgkin marginal zone B-cell or diffuse large B-cell lymphoma). Suspect lymphoma when a Hashimoto patient presents with a rapidly enlarging painless neck mass.

8. Surgical Indications in Hashimoto's Disease

Surgery (total thyroidectomy) is reserved for large compressive goiters causing dyspnea/dysphagia, co-existing suspicious nodules, or cosmetic disfigurement.

9. High-Yield Review & Board Exam Memory Anchors

  • Histologic Feature: Germinal centers & Hürthle (Askanazy) cells.
  • Lymphoma Risk: 60-fold increased risk of B-cell thyroid lymphoma.
  • Primary Antibodies: Anti-TPO (>95%).
CHAPTER 8

Postpartum & Silent Painless Thyroiditis

8.1

Triphasic Course & Clinical Management

1. Introduction and Clinical Definition

Postpartum thyroiditis is a variant of silent lymphocytic thyroiditis occurring within 12 months of parturition due to rebound immune activation post-delivery.

2. Epidemiology and Risk Factors

Affects 5–10% of postpartum women. Risk is significantly elevated in women with positive pre-pregnancy TPOAb titers or type 1 diabetes mellitus.

3. Triphasic Clinical Course Dynamics

Postpartum thyroiditis follows a classic triphasic course: transient thyrotoxicosis (1–2 months), followed by hypothyroidism (2–6 months), and eventual euthyroid recovery in 80% of cases.

4. Thyrotoxic Phase (Follicle Destruction Kinetics)

The initial thyrotoxic phase results from immune destruction of follicles releasing preformed T4/T3 into circulation, not active hormone synthesis.

5. Hypothyroid Phase Kinetics

Following colloid depletion, patients enter a hypothyroid phase manifesting fatigue, weight gain, constipation, and depression.

6. Diagnostic Differentiation (RAIU & Serology)

Nuclear RAIU shows low/absent uptake (<1%), definitively distinguishing destructive thyroiditis from true hyper-synthesizing Graves' disease (which has elevated uptake).

7. Medical Management Protocols

Thyrotoxic symptoms are managed with beta-blockers (Propranolol). Thionamides are ineffective because synthesis is absent. L-T4 is administered temporarily during the hypothyroid phase.

8. Long-Term Recurrence and Hypothyroidism Risk

Although 80% recover euthyroid function, 20% develop permanent hypothyroidism or experience recurrence in future pregnancies.

9. High-Yield Review & Triphasic Summary

  • Clinical Course: Thyrotoxic (1-2 mo) -> Hypothyroid (2-6 mo) -> Euthyroid.
  • RAIU Scintigraphy: Low/absent radioactive iodine uptake (<1%).
  • Treatment Warning: Thionamides are contraindicated; use Beta-blockers.

Acute Suppurative & Riedel's Fibrous Struma

9.1

Suppurative Fistulas & IgG4 Riedel Decompression

1. Acute Suppurative Thyroiditis Pathophysiology

Acute suppurative thyroiditis is a rare bacterial infection of the thyroid gland (Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes) protected by its high iodine content and rich vascular encapsulation.

2. Congenital Pyriform Sinus Fistula Remnants

SURGICAL PEARL — PEDIATRIC RECURRENT THYROIDITIS Recurrent left-sided suppurative thyroiditis in children is caused by a congenital pyriform sinus fistula derived from a persistent 3rd or 4th branchial pouch remnant. Definitive cure requires endoscopic fistulotomy or open excision of the tract during quiescent phases.

3. Suppurative Abscess Diagnostic Workup & Drainage

Patients present with fever, severe neck pain, erythema, and dysphagia. Workup includes ultrasound, FNA Gram stain/culture, IV antibiotics, and abscess drainage.

4. Subacute Granulomatous (de Quervain's) Thyroiditis

Subacute granulomatous thyroiditis is a post-viral inflammatory disorder presenting with severe anterior neck pain radiating to the jaw, hyperthyroidism, markedly elevated ESR (>50 mm/hr), and granulomatous multinucleated giant cells on histology.

5. Riedel's Fibrous Struma Pathology & IgG4 Association

Riedel's thyroiditis is a rare IgG4-related systemic sclerosing disease characterized by dense fibrous tissue replacing thyroid parenchyma and invading adjacent neck structures.

6. Clinical Presentation of Riedel Struma

Presents as a "woody" or rock-hard, fixed neck mass causing severe tracheal compression, dysphagia, and recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, mimicking anaplastic carcinoma.

7. Diagnostic Evaluation and Biopsy Confirmation

Core needle or open biopsy is required to confirm dense hyalinized fibrous tissue with chronic inflammatory infiltrates, ruling out anaplastic cancer or lymphoma.

8. Medical & Surgical Decompression Management

Medical therapy includes high-dose systemic corticosteroids and Tamoxifen. Surgery is limited to thyroid isthmusectomy to relieve tracheal compression; total thyroidectomy is contraindicated due to loss of anatomical planes and extreme risk of nerve/parathyroid injury.

9. High-Yield Review & Infectious/Fibrous Board Summary

  • Pediatric Recurrent Abscess: Left pyriform sinus fistula (3rd/4th pouch).
  • de Quervain Hallmark: Painful tender thyroid, high ESR, post-viral.
  • Riedel Surgical Procedure: Isthmusectomy only (tracheal decompression).
